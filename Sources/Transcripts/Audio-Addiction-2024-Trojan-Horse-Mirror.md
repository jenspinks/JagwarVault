**Interviewer:** How's it going, guys — welcome to Audio Addiction. We have Jagwar Twin with us today. Can you say your name and what you do in the band?

**Roy:** My name is Roy and I am Jagwar Twin.

**Interviewer:** All right, Roy, thank you so much. I also want to give a shout-out to Eder for getting us acquainted here — big salutes. I had the opportunity to listen through a good chunk of your discography while I was at the gym earlier today, and I really enjoyed it. It's a group I probably wouldn't have found otherwise, so it was cool to get you on my radar. I know you also released a new track called "Life Is Good," so I'll leave a link down in the description. But Roy, I wanted to know how Jagwar Twin started out — I'm kind of a huge band nerd, so I always love to know how people get their musical journey started. How did you start creating under the name Jagwar Twin?

**Roy:** I had done a lot of music in the past — I played in bands all growing up, and I did a bunch of music solo. But Jagwar Twin, even though it is a solo project, was sort of me coming to this place where I wanted to explore polarity. I wanted to explore light and dark. And the light and dark — I think I saw it first in the world around me, but then realized that that was a reflection of my internal world. So Jagwar Twin is really the exploration of, first, my internal world's light and dark, and the reflection of that in the external.

**Interviewer:** I found that a lot of your music — especially when I looked at the lyricism for "Life Is Good" — I felt a lot of resonance with that one. It's very easy to look at people's social media and think, "Wow, they must be living the best life," and then internally we all go through those struggles. You write from the heart, which is always really cool to see. How crucial is that for your writing process — to be more open or natural with the way you relay your emotions to people?

**Roy:** The heart is the key, and as you observed, I do write from the heart — but I didn't always. When I started out, it was writing all from the heart, from feelings, from true emotions. But then it starts to be like, "Well, I've got to make money, I've got to make this a career." I think the trap a lot of artists can find themselves in is starting to write music from the head. And I found myself in that trap for a few years. It's a continual process to get out of the thoughts, to get out of the mind. The mind is a useful tool, of course, but it needs to be subservient to one's own heart and inner guidance system. So — the heart is crucial.

**Interviewer:** I love that. And on the complete opposite end of the scale, the music is very whimsical — I'd almost put it in the same lane as a little bit avant-garde in some ways. I really enjoyed the almost nursery-rhyme aspect to your writing style. I listened to everything from your first record to the most current stuff, and I noticed a musical direction — especially with the newer stuff — that's a little more whimsical. Was that something you came to deliberately? Was there something in your head where you thought, "Let's switch it up, let's do something different" with the recent singles?

**Roy:** That's a good question, Brandon. The lullaby / children's-song aspect has been such a fun exploration, because I've always loved the simplicity of that music. I worked with a producer called Jeff Bhasker — he was one of my early mentors — and he made me study this weird thing. He'd be like, "Study 'Twinkle Twinkle Little Star,' study nursery rhymes, and study why they're the most genius songs of all time." It's wild, because those simple songs are really the most complex. Take "Happy Face," for instance — it's a very childlike melody, but mathematically it's also very complex. The most complex mathematical equations usually look very simple, like Einstein's E=mc². That's not [expletive] simple, but he was a true genius in the way he could distill it into something you can remember.

With Jagwar Twin, I call it Trojan Horse music. The purpose of a chorus, the purpose of something to sing along with, is like a mantra. Take a song like "Happy Face" — you're saying, "Hey, put on a happy face and everything's okay," this positive mantra, but the music is pretty dark and twisted-sounding. We even did an experiment with AI when we first released the song in 2019: you'd go to deathofmygeneration.fun, and there was facial recognition — it's still up, you can go look at it. You look at dystopian images of the world, everything that's going on, and as you do, you have to hold a smile. At first that was the only way you could listen to the song, before it was released on DSPs. What that does is — when you're forced to smile for three and a half minutes, however long the song is — it does something to the neurology and the chemicals in your brain. So all the music is what I call Trojan Horse. And every Jagwar Twin song is tuned to 444 Hz, which is a healing frequency. So it's a lot of subconscious things, but the goal is to heal the planet.

**Interviewer:** I like the metaphor of using it as a Trojan horse. Going through your music, I was like, "This seems familiar," but I know I've never listened to it — there's something about it. Jumping into the music, especially "Life Is Good" and "Happy Face" — those two stuck out to me. It had a familiarity, but I know I'd never listened to Jagwar Twin before. I love that aspect of it being so normal to me but also foreign in the same way. The way you go about it — the avant-garde way that you write — makes it very easy for people to jump in; it doesn't seem like this monumental task. But if you're a dedicated listener, you're going to pick out stuff you probably wouldn't have gotten on a surface level. It's almost like an iceberg: the top half, you're like, "This is a really catchy song," but if you're a dedicated fan you might find things underneath where you go, "Holy [expletive], I didn't realize there's extra stuff going on in this song." And I love the contrast between the darkness you bring in the musicality and the lyricism — you have to juxtapose those. There are a lot of layers. I'd highly recommend people check out Jagwar Twin. Roy, I have to ask — for people who may not have heard of Jagwar Twin, what song would you recommend a new listener check out first?

**Roy:** I would check out "The Circle" first.

**Interviewer:** Okay — "The Circle."

**Roy:** "The Circle" is Jagwar Twin's Genesis story. And then I would say listen to "Happy Face" after that.

**Interviewer:** Okay. The ones I've listened to are "Life Is Good," "Happy Face," "Great Time to Be Human," "I Like to Party," and "Loser" — that was the most current one in my rotation. Especially with the newer stuff, you can tell there's a great hook immediately once you jump in. But even listening to "Loser," which is on the first record, there's still a great hook — but you have to dig a little deeper, which is great too, because I love getting into artists where I have to work for it a little. As you've gone on, I feel like the hooks got better and better. I'm excited to delve into the previous record — is it '22 or '23? I know it's a number.

**Roy:** 33.

**Interviewer:** Oh — 33. I'm off entirely. So, 33 — I'm going to give that a listen a little later today. In terms of influences, Roy, do you have any artists you saw on TV or heard on the radio that got the gears turning toward being a musician and wanting to write music?

**Roy:** I soaked in everything. I don't really have favorites, but I collect things from different people. I love David Bowie and Michael Jackson — the classics — and I'm a big John Lennon fan. Musically, I listen to so much weird world music; I've been on this Russian, Polish, German tip, and you can hear it in the music, especially the new stuff — accordion and tubas. I'm going even deeper into that with a lot of the music I'm releasing now. I love weird, obscure sounds and things that make me go "huh." Broadway music, too — Jagwar Twin has a sort of theatrical element to it.

**Interviewer:** I'd agree — there's that theatrical, Broadway feel, especially adding the accordion, which I was trying to figure out if it actually was an accordion. That melody line was so enthralling. I think that's what ties a lot of the newer stuff together — the way it feels old but also new because of how the songs are produced. The group I'd most assimilate your music to is Twenty One Pilots — that dark-pop feel. Obviously a lot of Bowie influence, a lot of older Michael Jackson influence, but to keep it more current, I'd say Twenty One Pilots. You take a lot of old inspiration and modernize it. The next question: who are you jamming to currently? What's on your Spotify?

**Roy:** Mostly sounds of waves, nature sounds, meditation sounds. That, and Kendrick.

**Interviewer:** Okay — any Kendrick album specifically?

**Roy:** Mr. Morale, the newest one. So good. And the new Fletcher single slaps.

**Interviewer:** I'll have to check that out — I haven't heard Fletcher before. Do you plan on incorporating more nature and soundscape elements into Jagwar Twin? Is that the next phase?

**Roy:** Probably not. Everything is nature — listening to the way the birds articulate their phrases, there's a lot of that in everything, in melodic structure. Listening to nature, you can learn a lot. You can learn a lot from listening to the wind.

**Interviewer:** I'd agree — even the ways birds communicate with each other have such a melodic quality. I'll have to dig deeper, go outside, listen to some more live noise out there. The next question is a fun one: if you could pick a song to cover, does anyone come to mind?

**Roy:** Prince.

**Interviewer:** Okay — any Prince song specifically?

**Roy:** "I Would Die 4 U" — one of my favorite songs of all time.

**Interviewer:** I feel like you could do a Prince song justice, for sure. The next question: what's your favorite food to eat — your go-to?

**Roy:** I don't have favorites of anything, but if you ask me right now — raspberries. Just plain raspberries, today, at this moment, at 11:18 PST. But organic raspberries only.

**Interviewer:** I got you. Hopefully next time I see you play live, I'll bring you some organic raspberries.

**Roy:** I would love that, Brandon. You got it.

**Interviewer:** The next question: I know you've done a lot of collaborations, especially with the newest song, "Life Is Good." If there was someone else you'd love to work with, does anybody come to mind?

**Roy:** There's an artist I discovered recently named Ren, out of the UK. He's really dope — maybe I'll do a song with him.

**Interviewer:** There you go. Speaking of that, I know you did a collaboration on "Life Is Good" with, I believe, Angel Baby. How did that come to be — was it mutual, were you fans of each other's music?

**Roy:** I heard about his music a couple of years ago when I first started getting into AI and Web3 and blockchain. Angel was doing really cool stuff in the metaverse / Web3 world, and my manager at the time, Linus, hooked it up because they knew each other. We got in the studio and just had fun — I didn't put any pressure on it, and the song turned out really good.

**Interviewer:** It is extremely catchy. I always describe a good hook as an earworm, and it's got that great melody line that makes it memorable. If you'd love to hear a collab with Jagwar Twin and Ren — follow them on Instagram, make a story tagging both, type "collab," and send it. You've got to put it out in the universe, manifest it. The next question: for people more into the Jagwar Twin weeds, what song do you feel deserves a little more love — an underrated gem?

**Roy:** "Soul Is A Star." That song — first of all, Joni Mitchell signed off on that song. Nobody knows this. To get that song out, I used this piece of music from Joni Mitchell, and this was when she'd pulled all her stuff off Spotify. Labels and publishers were trying to clear it for me to use, and they were like, "There's no way — Joni Mitchell doesn't allow anybody to use samples or interpolations, we're not even going to ask." Their label said this, the publisher said this. And I was like, "[expletive] that — if Joni Mitchell hears this song, she will understand it, and she'll know it." So, long story short — I found a friend who knew a friend who knew a friend who knew Joni Mitchell, and ended up getting the song to Joni. She loved the song, signed off on it easily. I wrote her a little note. It didn't really get a push — it doesn't fit a genre, and sometimes in this era, if it doesn't fit a Spotify playlist genre, there's nowhere to put it and it goes unnoticed. But that song will have a life somewhere. It was such an important song to write, and I'm glad it exists.

**Interviewer:** Oh my God. They don't have a lot of streams right now, but hopefully — I'm going to go give it a listen after this. That's such an incredible story. For Joni Mitchell to sign off on it is such a big deal. Like you said, she's not going to do that as a favor.

**Roy:** It was true artistry at work. There are two people who have done it — me and Janet Jackson.

**Interviewer:** That's some great company. So everybody's got to give that a listen now, for the sheer fact that Roy's in the same company as Janet Jackson. Love to see it. The next question: if you were to compile a dream tour lineup, who would be on that list?

**Roy:** That's a good one. For current bands, I would do Jagwar Twin, Twenty One Pilots, Yungblud, and Kendrick Lamar. That's the tour. I'd be about that.

**Interviewer:** I can envision that — that's a hot lineup, surprised it hasn't happened yet. The next question: if there was one song you wish you'd written, what comes to mind?

**Roy:** It really is "I Would Die 4 U" by Prince. I think that song is one of the most brilliantly written and crafted songs I've ever heard in my life. Prince is super underrated — obviously a monumental talent, but the songs he's known for have their popularity, well deserved, and then there's so much underrated stuff people don't give love to. Prince is harder to digest — you have to actively listen to Prince, read the lyrics and pay attention. Music now is more supplementary to something.

**Interviewer:** I agree to a degree. During my day job I'll listen to music and not really pay attention, because I need to keep my mind moving. But sometimes I'll throw a record on the player and sit down and savor it. I think it's the act of caring — listening to an artist you really want to dive into. How crucial is that for you, for people who listen to your music to sit down and savor it? Do your fans listen in-depth, or is it more surface-level?

**Roy:** Jagwar Twin fans are the coolest human beings ever. They're like a bunch of weird geniuses, all in their own different ways. Especially the last album, 33 — you have to put your headphones on, close your eyes, and listen to all the things going on, and you'll hear a totally different thing. Jagwar Twin fans — some know how to read Egyptian hieroglyphs, some are quantum-physics people, some are math geniuses, some are just intuitive geniuses. We have a Jagwar Twin Discord, and it's so amazing to talk in there, because it's such a unique group of people who are attracted to the music.

**Interviewer:** It's really cool to see what your reflection is, in a way — who you attract. Your music being as eclectic as it is, you attract that eclectic fan base. Even listening to only five or six songs, I got it — I understand the eclectic nature. I hope that if one song doesn't fit someone, they go listen to another, and if that doesn't resonate, that's fine, people can go live their lives. My goal with guests who might not fit my listening habits is to listen in-depth and understand why people love this music. For you, Roy, it's such a cool experience to delve into, and there's so much more I can get out of it the more I get into it. I'll have to join the Discord. The next question: what's one band you feel puts on a great live performance?

**Roy:** 5 Seconds of Summer. I love their shows — they're so fun, just like dance parties.

**Interviewer:** They're fantastic, great stage presence and live energy. It makes you feel happy inside. The next question: what's your favorite TV show and favorite movie?

**Roy:** Favorite TV show right now, if you ask me — the Dark Crystal, the new one on Netflix. Slaps. Codes. Favorite movie right now — Fight Club.

**Interviewer:** That's a classic, for good reason. I'll have to check out The Dark Crystal — on Netflix, right? The next question: if you're trapped on a desert island for the next month and could bring one album, what would it be?

**Roy:** Something by Coldplay.

**Interviewer:** Are you an OG Coldplay fan, or the newer stuff?

**Roy:** I like all Coldplay — every single album. I'd get the Coldplay Greatest Hits.

**Interviewer:** I'm an OG Coldplay fan — "Yellow" is one of those I can listen to front to back. There's something about their classic sound. I'll have to give the newer stuff some love too. The last question is a big one. I appreciate your time, but I wanted to know why you're passionate about making music. I love to know why artists do it. So, Roy — why do you love to do it, and why is it something you continue to evolve with?

**Roy:** My "why" for making music has evolved over the years. But my "why" is — I know darkness so well. I know deep depression and anxiety in myself. But I also know that you can get out of that. And my mission with music is to be a mirror — to be the clearest mirror that I can be, to remind people who they truly are. And that's a journey internally, in myself, because I have to do that every day. I have to dive into my psyche and my subconscious and my spirit and my soul, pull things out, beat myself up — it's like Fight Club, beat yourself up — but you do that, and then you can become a clearer reflection for others.

**Interviewer:** I appreciate that. I'm excited to delve more into your lyricism, but as I mentioned, there's a sense of honesty I was drawn to. It's a mirror — very self-reflective. It's easy for people to see one side of you online, a persona, and everybody carries that, but people forget there's a level of human quality underneath that a lot of people don't get to see — or if they do, it's within closed circles, among friends or family. I appreciate that there's a directness in the way you write, and I think that should be celebrated. Hopefully it gives people a reason to look inward and figure out the things they need to take a look at. Much kudos, Roy. But I also have to leave you with the last thing — I'll give you the floor. Tell them where they can find Jagwar Twin, what you have coming up, and anything else you'd like to plug.

**Roy:** You can find Jagwar Twin all over the worldwide web. Just type in "Jagwar Twin" — you know how to do it.

**Interviewer:** It's been a pleasure.

**Roy:** Thank you. My pleasure.

**Interviewer:** If you like Jagwar Twin, he has a new song called "Life Is Good," helped out by Angel Baby — go check that one out, links in the description. Go give him a follow on all the social media. I'm sure he has big stuff planned in 2024. Shout-outs to Eder again, major shout-outs to Kos Norra, much love. If you enjoyed this interview, share, like, and subscribe — it goes a long way. We're very close to hitting 4,000 subscribers. Huge thanks to Roy for taking the time.

**Roy:** My pleasure.
