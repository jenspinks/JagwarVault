# The Mirror Sessions — Jeff Ellis Worldwide on Mixing and Mastering Authenticity

*The Mirror Sessions*

**Jeff:** Thank you.

**Roy:** How do you feel in this exact moment?

**Jeff:** I'm thinking about what all had to take place to get to here from the first day you came into my studio.

**Roy:** Whoa.

**Jeff:** Because, you know, I hadn't seen you in a long time, and you wanted to show me something special. And you showed me something special, and now we're here, man.

**Roy:** I feel like you have this ability to see behind things, or maybe through people. Because even with Subject to Flooding, it was like I was still trying to figure so much out at that time, in the beginning of that. And I feel like you saw something that was there in me as an artist, but was kind of maybe a little bit buried, or yet to be totally uncovered. But you still dove in and helped bring that out.

There's been a few people in my life who have really seen me early on, and you're definitely one of those people who helped me believe in myself. And I feel like you also do that with a lot of artists. Through your discography, you look at it and there's a lot of artists you've been a part of their careers from really early on, and then seeing them become some of the biggest artists in the world. You got your Grammys. But I guess the question is: how do you see that, and what do you look for? Or is it intuitive?

**Jeff:** There's so much to humans that is unexplainable and is sometimes just a feeling. And if you try to intellectualize it, you just go around in circles and don't get anywhere.

But with you, I don't know. It's like… when I look at myself, you can always see an untapped potential. And with music, it's always about what is holding us back from within. What insecurities do we have? Where does that come from? Fears, things from when we were young. There's so much that goes into blocking yourself from being that true thing.

After seeing so many different projects succeed or fail, you kind of see a trend: the artists that are able to take who they are on the inside, what's inside their heart, and get it to come out of speakers in the most unfiltered way tend to make the music that impacts the most people.

Sometimes you can feel who someone is. You can feel their soul. You can feel their true unfiltered self before they're ready to even feel that or be that. And everybody has a timeline on when they're ready to be that person. Some people — it's super rare — but some people get that right out of the gate. That's so rare. And a lot of times that comes with all sorts of other problems.

But I think that when you can be that person who has to go on a long journey to reach that place, that's almost a more rewarding way to do it and a more exciting way to do it. And I'm on my own version of that, and you're on your own version of that. And the second that we think that we're there, we realize we're not, and we keep going. But eventually we learn to really enjoy the ride and the journey as opposed to fighting it every step of the way.

But to get back to the original question, it's just a feeling. I don't know what anything is. Especially every year I get older, I get more confused. But there's something about just feeling someone's soul, someone's energy. And that's kind of the most beautiful thing about getting to meet so many musicians and artists — that they have such radiant energy, and they're trying to find ways to express that and trying to get over their fears.

And when you see — I've seen a bunch of artists that I work with — have these periods of blossoming, that's one of the most beautiful things to see. It's just a blossoming to where it's like, "Oh, this is the real you. I didn't see that before. I knew something was probably in there." And a lot of times it happens fast. It takes forever, but then it happens really fast.

Right. Yeah, man. So we're here. We made it. The album's finished. It's not out yet, but it's like this — \[attribution uncertain: "Right. Yeah, man…" may be Roy — see note\]

**Roy:** For me as the artist, it felt like this period of blossoming, like you said, where it was like there had been so many years — I mean a decade, really, over a decade — of almost being veiled from myself, where I knew to a degree who I was, but I couldn't get what was inside out.

And getting back to what you said, so much of art and music and expression is getting what's inside out, and what makes you truly unique. And it's the hardest thing to do. Even now, the camera turns on and you're thinking about, "Is someone watching? Did I say something stupid? I love my hair. I got a zit on my face this morning."

**Jeff:** Yeah.

**Roy:** Or whatever. And it's like, well, you just gotta be—

**Jeff:** I got a zit too. Maybe on my face no one notices. And in fact, if they do, it makes them happy because—

**Roy:** Because, like, "I don't have a zit?"

**Jeff:** No, I think it's more like, "Okay, cool. They're just normal people."

**Roy:** Yeah. They're just people.

**Jeff:** But that's funny.

**Roy:** Wow, look at this view.

**Jeff:** Yeah, this place is gorgeous. How did you find this place?

**Roy:** My friend KK took me here once to take some photos.

**Jeff:** This is gorgeous. I like the way it smells. You can go a long time living in a city and never really just know how the world's supposed to smell.

**Roy:** I know, right? I think this is normal everywhere else. Dirt. What do you think is more difficult: jiu-jitsu or being on camera?

**Jeff:** Jiu-jitsu is — see, the thing about jiu-jitsu is it's a metaphor for life. So in a lot of ways it's the same thing.

**Roy:** But in what way?

**Jeff:** The camera is not trying to choke you.

**Roy:** But it is. \[attribution uncertain — see note\]

**Jeff:** It's all the same thing. When you get down to it, everything is the same thing. Jiu-jitsu, if you push too hard… if people are watching this and have no idea what it is: when it's in its sport form, it's about submitting another person with some sort of joint lock or choke or whatever, getting them to give up. And to do that, you generally have to use various techniques to trick them into letting their guard down so you can—

**Roy:** This sounds like social media to me.

**Jeff:** Yeah, exactly. I mean, it's a game, right? But it's a game where if you push too hard, you don't get the result you want. It's just like everything else.

If you can get into a flow state, you can see what others don't see. And while other people are wrapped up in fight-or-flight mode or some primitive part of their brain, you're in a flow state, so you can see and set up things and trick people into letting their guard down.

So just like being on camera, if you get in the flow state, it's irrelevant that the camera exists.

Why jiu-jitsu is a metaphor for life is because it's everything. Everything in life is that polarity, that whole balance of the universe. The yogis talk about union or unison with the whole universe. That's what jiu-jitsu trains you to find — that unison in the flow state in the most dire situations, where someone's trying to choke you or bend your arm backwards. So if you can find it there, you can find it on camera pretty quickly.

**Roy:** So I gotta do some jiu-jitsu.

**Jeff:** Well, it's just like when you put yourself way out of your comfort zone, that's where all the growth is, right? I'm sure that, you know, I'm at the point now when something bad happens to me, I'll feel really sad and terrible for like 40 seconds.

**Roy:** The road's often less traveled. \[attribution uncertain — see note\]

**Jeff:** I'll feel bad for like 30 or 40 seconds, and then I'll realize, "Oh, okay. Here's another amazing lesson." Because once you get into that phase where something terrible has happened and you level up significantly, you're like, "Okay, well something bad just happened. This is an opportunity to grow now."

**Roy:** Let's do it. What's that? Anyway, so… yeah, that was a great answer.

**Jeff:** Yeah, but if you're always in your comfort zone, you're not getting anywhere. You're not growing.

**Roy:** Yeah, absolutely. So it's like, how do we get out of our comfort zones while still maintaining a productive, happy life? Yeah, I mean, some of my favorite times in life — even right now — there's so much that's out of my comfort zone, but I love it. I don't know, it's kind of weird. Even going through a hard breakup, it was like a simultaneous most sorrow I've ever felt, but also the most joy and the most I've ever learned.

**Jeff:** I mean, that's your greatest period of growth, right? So yeah, buckle up. It's time to grow. And once that happens enough in your life, you start to realize, "Oh, this is part of the design," you know what I mean?

**Roy:** Yeah, yeah.

**Jeff:** And then you stop seeking a life of comfort where everything goes right. Because when everything goes wrong, all of a sudden six months later, you're closer to the version of yourself that you always wanted to be. Now, it is very much possible to go the other way.

**Roy:** Yeah. And people downward spiral.

**Jeff:** And people do that all the time, for sure. The downward spiral is a possibility. But an upward spiral is a possibility too. It's the goal. So if we can admit that the downward spiral exists, then we also have to admit the upward spiral exists as well. And it's all a spiral.

**Roy:** The upward spiral is the goal. Incubus had a song called "Spiral."

**Jeff:** Maybe that's what that was about.

**Roy:** Maybe. That's funny how different songs, as you get older and you go through things in life—

**Jeff:** Oh my gosh.

**Roy:** —and they take on a whole other meaning.

**Jeff:** Yeah. And it's like, "Oh, okay, well…" And that's one of the things we were talking about with your album in the beginning: these layered songs, where the surface-level interpretation of the song is great, the mid-level interpretation of the song is great, and then depending on where someone is in their life, there might even be a different meaning, a deeper meaning, that is completely personal to them. That's like — and unique to them.

**Roy:** That's my favorite kind of music, though — songs that have layers and layers and layers. You can meet it on the surface level, or you can meet it on this really deep level.

I was doing some radio promo, and one of the DJs — is that what they call them still? DJs? Disc jockeys? One of the disc jockeys was like, "Oh, my favorite song's 'I Like to Party.'" And I was like, "Oh, that's awesome. What do you like about it?" She's like, "Oh no, I just like to party. Like me and my cats." And I was like, "Yes. I love that. I love everything about that." Because it is that too, you know? And then in my mind, like, "Oh, it's a song about political division," and whatever. I love it — layers.

**Jeff:** When you're writing, how do you do that thing where you can hit lyrics that kind of branch off into all these different layers? Where does that come from? Is that — I mean, you've been working on that craft for a while. Is that something you've tried to develop over your life, or did it just kind of arrive there somehow?

**Roy:** It all kind of shifted when I changed the reason I was making music.

Because when I first started making music, I was in junior high and I didn't really fit in with the other kids. I got bullied and made fun of. And I was like, "Well, I'll do something that would try to make me cool and get girls to like me. I'm gonna learn how to make music." And that was my root motivation. It was as simple as that.

And then I kind of got stuck and lost in that. I had a couple different major label deals, as you know, before. Each time it was like the cycle that would repeat, where I'd get signed and be like, "All right, now the music that I'm gonna make is gonna be super successful. This is what people want. Here's what's popular at the time," whatever. Because the only motivation was to be successful, really, or have people like me, make money, whatever. Nothing wrong with any of those things, but it's a base motivation.

And then after a while it was like, what are you really doing? Who are you really? Why are you on the planet?

I took a few years to really look at that and examine my purpose and my motivation, and looked into myself, and kind of started my spiritual journey. Yeah. I guess, I mean, you're always on your spiritual journey whether you know it or not.

**Jeff:** Whether you know it or not.

**Roy:** I think changing that and being like, "Well, I want to, with whatever I do, help humanity in some way, or show human beings the reflection of themselves back to themselves." Because I know what it's like to be lost. I know what it's like to not know yourself and feel like you're alone. But I know that if I can do it, anybody can do it.

**Jeff:** Yeah. And so I feel like when that shifted so much, then in some ways your subconscious kind of takes over, or your higher self, or however you want to personify it. You get into that state of flow and you turn off the thinking mind, and then these things come out and you're not thinking about the words. You're just writing them down or saying them as they come. And then you look back and you're like, "Whoa. Yeah. This is awesome." So you weren't even writing?

**Roy:** I wasn't even — I was just channeling something. I was just the vehicle. And in the past I always heard artists say that and I was like, "Wow, you're so cool." And I was like, no, that really happens. And it's so beautiful to take yourself out of the equation.

**Jeff:** It would be funny if somehow, through some temple vortex, we could have a photo of this moment back when you came into my studio to show me the unmixed version of the album. We would have been like, "Damn, something wild happened between now and then." \[uncertain: "temple vortex" — see note\]

**Roy:** Dude, something really shifted, though, in that time. And even me reaching out in the first place was weird. I had this feeling — like that voice in your head that talks to you—

**Jeff:** Oh, there's like a lot of voices maybe.

**Roy:** But like the positive voice that talks to you in your head.

**Jeff:** Yeah.

**Roy:** I've been learning to listen to that more. And it's usually very illogical. And so that day when I reached out to you, it was like, "Yo, hit Jeff up." And I was like, "Oh, he's probably so busy. I haven't talked to him in a long time. I don't know if he actually likes me," whatever. And it was like, "No, just say hi. Say what's up."

And I was like, okay, I'm gonna listen to you, voice in my head.

And so I just did. And then you were like, "Oh yeah, I actually have a slower day at the studio." And you were at the new spot at EastWest, close to my house.

**Jeff:** Was this the same day you hit me up? I was like, "Come through."

**Roy:** This was the same day. Yeah. And I went through not with an intention of anything. I was like, "Hey, hang with Jeff."

And hearing your reaction to the music that was played was like, "Oh, maybe this really is something." Because it was so in my own world for a long time — all the songs and all the music. And it was weird. I played you a song that starts, it's like two minutes of a cappella — "Long ago there was a jaguar" — but you saw it and you heard it. And it was pretty cool to feel that and have someone believe in it.

**Jeff:** Yeah, that album came to me at the exact right moment that I needed it in my own personal life. And it was like, "Oh, this is something that I have to work on." It wasn't even my choice. Normally I just decide, "I'm gonna work on this album or I'm not." This one was like, "Oh, this has been decided for me, so let's just figure out how we can make this happen."

Because all the songs — it arrived just on time for me. So if it's arriving just on time for me, it needs to arrive just on time for a lot of other people. And then I'm like, I'm the only person that can do this album as a mixer. I really truly believe that. So if it's not me, then it's going to be a disservice to all the people that need it. But it's also going to be me just ignoring the signs, which are everywhere if you start believing in magic, I guess you could say.

**Roy:** Dude, straight up, man. Even today, doing this, it was like I got here at 5:55. I was like, I don't know. You could see those things as coincidences or you could not. It doesn't matter either way, but I think the signs are always there.

**Jeff:** It's a much better way to live, believing.

**Roy:** And it's so much more fun.

**Jeff:** It's more fun. Like, so much more. Even if it's just that. The whole definition of the game is you never really get any answers, but you get some signs along the way. We start as children believing in magic, and then we let go of that. And we focus on science and math and everything, and eventually you can answer every question except for why.

And then science falls flat on its face when it gets to the why question. And then that's when you have to start believing in magic again, if you're trying to get any — not answers — but comfort, I guess.

So yeah, that was the magic moment when you came in and you played me this album, and I heard exactly what it needed to be at the end. And I was listening to it on my drive up here today, and I was like, "This is what it is. This is what it had to be." And now we're on top of this hill.

**Roy:** There's been a lot of beautiful life changes through this whole process. I'm so grateful to you, my friend.

**Jeff:** Likewise.

**Roy:** For real, likewise. It means so much that you saw it before anybody did.

**Jeff:** Yeah. The cooler thing about getting older, at least for me personally, is the amount of times I didn't trust my instincts and I was actually right far outweigh the amount of times I trusted my instincts and I was wrong. But you just have to get to the point where you're filtering out what is an insecure thought, and then what is a true thought from within. And that just comes — that's what wisdom is.

**Roy:** Yeah.

**Jeff:** Wisdom is earned through pain.

**Roy:** It's true. So it has to be some sacrifice.

**Jeff:** I was thinking about, if you look at life as the best game to ever play — I was thinking about this yesterday — when I was a kid I would play Super Mario Bros., and how lame would that game be if you couldn't fall off the map or you couldn't get swallowed up by some creature?

**Roy:** No point.

**Jeff:** There would be no point. That's what makes everything exciting. So even with mixing music, the second any kind of fear crap creeps into you, that's your insecurity, and now you're out of a flow state and you're not doing the right thing anymore.

**Roy:** Yeah. Seeing you in the studio mixing is so inspiring. The way you do it is so different than any other mixer. It really is flow, and you trust your heart, you trust your gut. It's like you're playing an instrument when you're standing up, and you feel that thing. And sometimes you make weird decisions, but it just feels awesome.

**Jeff:** Yeah. It took me a long time to get there, and I'm still trying to get there further — sharpening my sword. Every attempt it gets a little sharper. But that all came from a place of seeing the difference between listening and feeling.

When we're making music, almost everybody gets into this place of listening. "I'm listening for something that could be wrong," right? Instead of feeling what could be right.

And so much of when we're listening to our favorite music and our favorite songs, we're just feeling. Things that would otherwise seem like rule-breaking or dramatic or might seem broken — when we're just feeling it, it feels right.

Things that catch us off guard when we're listening or thinking don't necessarily catch us off guard when we're feeling. And to have heavy emotion invoked in you, something's got to catch you off guard or be out of balance.

A lot of the classic records, it'll be like a guitar that's like — yeah — out of nowhere.

**Roy:** Yeah. And you love it.

**Jeff:** Yeah. And that's what gets you playing air guitar.

**Roy:** Playing air guitar, yeah.

**Jeff:** Because it throws you out of your passive state. But if you were to listen to that under the veil of, "Oh, is this song right? Is this mix right?" you might be like, "Oh, I don't know, that caught me off guard. Is it gonna be jarring?" Meanwhile, the person that is just feeling the song — not thinking about audio production or music production — is playing air guitar all of a sudden.
