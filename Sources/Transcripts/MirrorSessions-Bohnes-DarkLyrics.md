Roy: Actually, that's a good question. What is your purpose at this point in your life, making music? Actually, let's go back: why did you start, and then how has it changed?

Bohnes: I started making music purely out of the need and want, just hanging out with my friends. It was typical — like, I was 14. I was listening to The Used and AFI and Rancid, and I was like, man, this music is just my passion, I just loved it so much. I wouldn't eat lunch, I'd just starve at school and save up my money for concert tickets. And you know, that's cool — naturally you meet people who have the same interests and the same music, and then we honestly just started playing completely for fun. We just wanted to hang out, it's just like something to do. I never was like, "One day I'm gonna sign a record deal." No, never.

Roy: Didn't cross your mind?

Bohnes: Never crossing. See, that was my reason. Really?

Roy: Yeah, mine was pure ego. Started because I was like, "I want people to like me, I want to be really cool." I wanted to be a pro basketball player. Well, that wasn't gonna happen.

Bohnes: It wasn't. Let's see — you can see Santa Monica up here.

Roy: Oh yeah.

Bohnes: Why do you make music now? While you keep going.

Roy: Um, I think honestly, in a weird way, music is pretty selfish for me. I think it's kind of like an itch that I have to scratch, or I go crazy, you know. It's something I just need to get off my chest, and I just love being creative and building these worlds. I've even found that music has always kind of filled in holes and gaps for me — so when I was really sad, then I wrote my happiest songs, and then when I was really happy, I would write really sad songs. And I always thought that was really interesting. When I was really sad, I could never write about it. It was like I was too deep in the emotion. So I'd always almost write what I wish I was feeling right now.

Bohnes: Like, a lot of your music content is pretty dark, and I'm happy.

Roy: Yeah, yeah, you're like so happy.

Bohnes: It's always been like this dichotomy for me. I'm not really sure why. And then, you know, I think once I realized that music would be such a vessel to inspire others, or at least just to show others that there's someone that can relate to them, or that feels the same way as they do — that kind of was really beautiful for me. And then it kind of gave me a purpose, you know. I was like, "Oh wow, I can not only do this for myself, but there's a kid out there that needed to hear that they weren't alone in something, or that someone else kind of felt or had the same thoughts."

Roy: And you're always there to catch people. \[Laughter\] Yeah, man. I mean, there's layers to it, you know. I feel like music is such a way to explore parts of yourself. I feel like that's almost, in some ways, the purpose of it — it's like, to explore an unknown aspect of who you are as a human, and everyone's expression of that is different. Like, maybe — at least for me — times I've explored dark subject matter has been like, "Well, I didn't really understand that part of myself and I needed to figure that out in some way, shape, or form," or process something.

Bohnes: Yeah, you find corners of your mind that you didn't know existed.

Roy: Yeah, exactly, because there's some things you can't put into words. Like, even if we were to have a conversation, it doesn't matter how deep you can possibly go with whatever language you happen to speak — there's feelings that you feel, and music can put a word — like, the lyrics are sort of coordinates, you know. You say these words that are like, "I feel sad," but you can put "I feel sad" over really happy music, or you could put it over really dark music, or varying degrees of emotion, and it feels totally different ways. And that's something that's super powerful to explore — the power that's beyond language.

Bohnes: Yeah, I've always loved — I mean, obviously, you know, your song "Happy Face," right? It's kind of, uh — not ironic, but ironic in a way. Totally. And so I've always also loved the idea of putting a happy lyric, or even a happier-sounding melody, over minor chords. Or vice versa, right? Putting a dark song over major chords, and something that kind of rips your soul apart, because you're feeling two emotions at once. Because your instinct is to find joy in major chords, yes, but then when you're listening to a dark lyric, it's like two hands gripping your soul.

Roy: Yeah, yeah, that is something that's really beautiful. It's like a collision. For me, like with "Happy Face," for example — putting these weird sort of psychotic — this music bed was like psychotic music over really happy lyrics, it does something really interesting psychologically. Because, at least for me, one of the reasons I make music still now is, I'm aware of what it's like to be super depressed or super anxious. And like, you could say, "Hey, put on a happy face" over really major, happy chords, but it's not going to reach anybody that needs to hear that. It's almost like you're preaching to the choir. But if you put it over something dark, the music is what pulls out your emotions, but then it doesn't matter what you're feeling, in a sense. But if you're singing "Hey, put on a happy face," and you're smiling while you sing that, it does something psychologically that almost — it's like going into the darkness and pulling you out of it. And that's part of what we did with the "Happy Face" experiment when we first dropped the song — it was like, you had to look at dystopian images and smile for three and a half minutes, and then it changes the makeup in your body.

Bohnes: Yeah, and there's something so powerful that music can do. A lot of your songs do that too, like especially some of the darker ones — but like, you're singing it with a smile on your face, and you can hear the joy in your singing whatever dark lyric. And I love that.

Roy: It's a joy in the chaos.

Bohnes: Yeah. And I think maybe because, you know, I wasn't that wild of a kid. Like, I didn't get in that much trouble. I got pretty good grades — like, little kid, even, dude. In high school I had a 4.2 in high school. I didn't have a sip of alcohol until I graduated high school. Like, kids were smoking weed behind the gym after basketball practice, and I'm home with my guitar nerding out over guitar chords. So I think that once I started getting into my 20s, I wanted to explore chaos — whether that's musically, or whether that's just in life.

Roy: No, you were the influence — we were under the influence.

Bohnes: So again, it's like filling in those gaps, you know — like, "I haven't done that with my life, so I want to do that musically or sonically." It's like, again, just filling in space. It's like this whole pie, and there's pieces cut out, and you just want to see what it's like when that whole pie is full.

Roy: Yeah. Whole, like — on the pie of what makes you a human. Because everyone's rounded, you know. So if you have jagged edges, that's temporary, or it's just shining a light on only a piece of you. It's like a movie.

Bohnes: Let me see Skull Rock — like three minutes.

Bohnes: I still want to do a Jaguar trip with you.

Bohnes: Go to the — and all in Brazil, and we see them in the future. I mean, I'm seeing one in the flesh now, but you know, gotta see your familia.

Roy: I gotta see the family. I actually feel very connected with the jaguar.

Bohnes: Yeah, what — what do you do, that —

Roy: I think, like, you as an artist are always expanding. Um, but this album — I guess I would describe it — it's called 33, and I made it when I was 33.

Bohnes: What are you doing, the Adele? Yeah, it's just like Adele's albums, it's basically the same. Expect a few grandkids. Yeah, you know, whatever the panel of Grammy people say really matters.

Roy: So, look, this is part of it, but Skull Rock I think is the one right behind it. Oh, but it's a really cool viewpoint.

Bohnes: Good answer.

Roy: I've always loved nature and exploration, and, you know, green representing just like Earth. And I wanted to be Indiana Jones, you know — wanted to stumble upon some grand adventure, some cool natural enterprise.

Bohnes: Green actually makes so much sense. And also, it's like a heart color, and you're very connected with your heart.

Roy: Is it a heart color? Yeah, yeah. And nature, and all those things.

Bohnes: You know, I noticed, like, sometimes looking in reverse, things make more sense. And so, like, I remember the first time ever encountering you — at Bamboozle. Actually, that's not true, it was a Chain Reaction, and you played with Fall Out Boy. Like, wasn't it 2007? I can't believe you were there.

Roy: It was — that was 2008, I think.

Bohnes: Eight, yeah, 2008. Yeah, if I didn't meet you then, I just saw the show, it's insane. And then probably that same year was Bamboozle, I think, or maybe the year after, was when I actually met you. But my first — I was like, "Who's this guy?" And then we became friends, like, more on the tour we went on together. But something I noticed about you — and it's interesting, even just like green and your heart — was you always had such a heart for people, and you could connect with somebody on a soul level pretty easily and deeply. And I noticed, like, as we became friends more — and especially around that — like, we were friends for almost a decade-ish. Yeah. And I noticed especially at that time, I was, like, a lot of anxiety and depression creeping in and out, and I would notice that — I don't know if you even were aware of this — but we'd be out somewhere, like, if I ever felt that way, you'd just go and put your arm around me, like, "Hey," you would say something nice. And I was like, whoa. And that was — I didn't notice it then, but I noticed it looking backwards, that you have that gift of making people feel very comfortable.

Roy: Well, I appreciate that. I feel like I have this empath thing about me, and I think it's like a blessing and a curse, because I feel everything. And that can really be a hindrance, or that can really be like a superpower, depending on how, when you feel it. But it's like, man, I can get sucked into a black hole if someone else is hurting. I'm like — right there, you know. The bullet holes are in me, you know, when I see it in someone else. And then, you know, I try, again, filling in those gaps.

Bohnes: Um, you know, you're talking about looking back — it's like you almost have this jaguar thing about you too, where when I look back at, like, when I met you, you're always eating an apple. And you know, dude, you —

Roy: A lot of appleless trees in the day, because I'm a killer, dude.

Bohnes: It was, like, your thing. Like, if someone drew a stick figure and an apple in the stick figure's hand, it'd be like, "Roy — that's him." Backpack and an apple — nope, Johnny Appleseed. And you always had positive energy, even if you're saying you're feeling other things, but you were also very observant. That's still you to this day. You're one to let everything soak — you soak everything in, and you take everything, and you're like a listener. And you almost — you're like a watcher too, and in a beautiful way. Like, you like to just exist, and you like to view, and you like to, again, just soak it in. And so, in a jaguar sense, it's like you are kind of in the jungle, in the bush, and you're watching, and you're taking care, and then when you come out, it's like this beautiful being and beautiful sight. And that's a jaguar, dude.

Roy: I love that, man, thank you.

Bohnes: And you take it in, and then — you notice things about people that they don't notice about themselves. Like, your observation and your emotional thought process is so beyond intelligent, what you see in people. And again, that's such a gift. Like, I think you can really bring the best out of people, because you can see things that they can't, you know. There's like layers that you're blind to in a way — that the person themselves are blind to, and that you have, like, x-ray vision.

Roy: Don't you think — you said "beyond intelligent" — because I feel like everybody has this capacity that is beyond intelligence. It's more like heart intelligence, or like emotional intelligence. Sometimes you can't put it into words, but you see things. And I feel like I've really worked on cultivating that, and being comfortable in silence, and being comfortable in watching and observing. Because sometimes, like you said, you feel everything — you feel so much, and that's probably one of the reasons why we're artists, is to try to make sense of that in some way. But when you feel so much and observe so much, at least for me, sometimes it was like, "Well, I don't know what to say, because there's not words to articulate this." So in the past I would feel really anxious about that, because I wasn't necessarily aware of all the things that I was feeling — or that it was maybe not me, it was coming from somebody else, but I was observing it. And I really worked on being comfortable just being, and observing, instead of, like, in high school being — it's like, "Oh, you're shy." It's like, I was never actually shy, it was just like, I'm observing at the moment, and if I don't have something to say, then nothing needs to be said.

Bohnes: I need to be better. Josephine was talking to me the other day, and she's just like, "You have to learn to be okay with silence, and with being bored, and being by yourself." And I'm really bad at those things, and she's dead-on. It's like — I mean, I haven't been to therapy in a long time, but if I were to go to a therapist, the first thing I would talk about is, like, why do I always need more? Right? It's like, why is there always a higher mountain to climb? And it's funny, it's never — I don't feel that way career-wise, like, I'm very content where I am. I don't need to be famous or a superstar, it's not in that way. It's just getting my soul excited. The older I get, it takes more and more, and I can't stand it about myself, you know. It's like, I've never been content with where I am. There's always another place to go, there's always another person to meet, there's always another song to write. And I want to be okay with sitting right here by myself for a few hours, and just letting my thoughts do whatever they want, without me being like, "I should be doing something else, I want to do something else."

Roy: You know, it's funny, because I think one of the reasons we've become friends is, we're almost the same, but in a mirrored reflection. I feel like I've learned so much from you, in that other way, of like going out on journeys, and exploring, doing crazy \[censored\], and like, you know — it's beautiful. And I guess every superpower also has its weakness, and we're all just working from different angles.

Bohnes: I think it's just important to — if you could realize that about yourself, then you can work on it, identify it. And I think that's probably my growth in the past year or so, is understanding that I do that — that I always am looking forward to the next adventure, instead of being present in the current adventure. I have anxiety right now because I don't have a trip coming up, like I don't have an adventure coming. And so I spin, I go down this black hole or rabbit hole of — like, the solemn — like, my wife is out of town, I moved far away from my friends, and my worst nightmare is being in a house by myself for like a month. Like, nothing could be worse. I remember I did a trip a few years ago to Italy, and I was about to start writing a new album, and I'm like, "Joe, you know what, I think I'm gonna go to Florence by myself for a few days, I'll go to some museums, I'm just gonna enjoy, I'm gonna go to my favorite restaurants, this is going to be the best few days of my life." Dude, eight hours in, I'm spiraling. I call Joe, "I can't do this, I can't do this, I'm freaking out, I have no one to talk to." So I actually called my friend at the time who lived in Belgium, and I'm like, "Sean, can you please come to Florence? Like, I need a friend, I can't be by myself." And I want to know why that is.

Roy: Dude, see, a month in a house by myself — a dream. \[Laughter\]

Bohnes: That's amazing. I just — yeah, I just love people so much. I love interaction, I love, you know — every person's like a labyrinth, and like a puzzle to figure out. I love that aspect of life, like meeting someone new and trying to unlock them is just so — it's one of like — every person is an adventure of sorts. And I always love adventure, so it's like, whether that's a physical adventure, like me going someplace to just get out of my comfort zone, or whether it's meeting someone new, it's like my favorite thing in the world. So I love making friends, because there's so much to get to know and to find out about people, that I almost know too much about myself at this point. I don't need more alone time, but — surely, to like, meet someone else where they're at, to unlock someone else, like, "What's this person all about?" — you have to know yourself pretty deeply to be able to do that, whether you're aware of that or not.

Roy: I think the beautiful thing about it too is, like, friendship — and even, like, being married, like with Josephine — it's like, people change and evolve in their life, so you never really solve the puzzle, right? Because people are constantly changing.

Bohnes: So much. Exactly. So by the time you think you've gotten to know somebody, or all that time you put in to get to know them or to feel them, it's like, well, they've probably changed a little bit, so there's more to know. And, you know, people grow — it's like vines, right? Sometimes maybe they grow parallel, and then sometimes they'll do this and go their separate ways, and then maybe they come twist around. That's kind of everyone in your life. And the older you get, the more you see that, man. Like, being in my 30s now, people are going through completely different things than they were 10 years ago, and they're completely different people — some for the better, some for the worse, some maybe not better or worse, just different, you know. And that's been a pretty big part of my 30s, is just seeing the evolution of people. You know, when you're a kid, it's like you're either not paying attention, or you're subconsciously seeing people change and grow. But when you're aware, and you've lived a lot of life, you really start to notice and pick up on those things.
