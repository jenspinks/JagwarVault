## ★ What this actually is — a documented method, mid-execution `[W]`

**This is not simply an early draft. It is a recording of Roy's own compositional process at the step he has described** — and it caught the step that leaves no trace in a finished song.

Jen's account of the method (2026-08-01, `[P]` — provenance note below): he starts from **a hook or a phrase alone**, with no other lyrics; he **sings nonsense into the melody** to build the structure around that hook; then he goes back and writes the real words. Sometimes the nonsense survives into the release — her example is **"flip the stove."**

Every feature of this recording is that method leaving fingerprints:

| in the recording | what it is |
|---|---|
| **"kut tow, kut tow … kut tow"** | ★ **nonsense — a placeholder structural sound**, holding the melodic slot where *"She wanted Love / But she got Fame"* would later be written. **Not** a lyric, and **not** a studio instruction |
| **the tempo loss** across those syllables | the tell. He drops time *exactly* where the words don't exist yet — he is working it out in real time, not performing |
| **the back-up and re-run** of *"when the world ain't what it seems, you know"* | a line being rehearsed into place, second pass more confident than the first |
| **the stumble** at the chorus turn | the same, on the part that matters most |
| **"Bang bang"** | ✅ **confirmed present** — and absent from the release |

⇒ **Reframed.** The first draft of this page called "Bang bang" and "cut talk" *"two lines that never reached the record."* That was wrong about the second one and wrong about the category. **"Bang bang" is a line that did not survive. "Kut tow" is scaffolding — it was never a line at all**, and the fact that it *didn't* survive is the method working normally, not an editorial decision. Conflating the two would invent a deletion that never happened.

⚠ **A correction I owe this page:** the first draft offered "a literal studio instruction caught on the take" as a live reading of *"cut talk."* **Dead.** It came from hearing English in a syllable that isn't English, which is the machines' failure mode reproduced in my prose.
