# Great Time to Be Human

## Album / Project
- [[Lucius Lullaby (album)]] — **track 3**. Five writers (Roy English, Ryan Spraker, Tom Peyton, Sean Van Vleet, Josh Dun) — the most-collaborative track on the album; Josh Dun (Twenty One Pilots) on drums, little luna on background vocals; vocals credited to **"ROY" and "sir lucius."**

> **One-line reading:** the album's **toxic-positivity anthem.** Lucius stages human history as a children's sing-along: the verses tell nursery-rhyme fables of grief, smothering love, ritual noise, institutional violence, predation, and botched god-making — and the refrain papers every one of them over with rainbows, butterflies, bliss, and forced gratitude. The horror is obvious. **The cruelty is in the cheer.**

## The refrain is the violence

It would be easy to call this a dark song with a happy chorus. It is exactly the reverse: a **happy chorus that is the dark thing.** The verses do their grotesque work — a brother smothered, a girl committed and shaved, bloodsuckers in crocodile shoes — and each time the song answers not with grief but with *"Rainbows, butterflies / what is all this? / follow your bliss / everything keeps blooming / what a great time to be human!"* The cruelty isn't in the events. It's in the **smile forced over them** — the ideology that insists you stay grateful while it shows you the wreckage. `[Strong Read.]`

That places it precisely among its album-siblings. [[Lucius Lullaby]] *confesses* the lie (*"it's a beautiful life… but it's all just a lie"*); [[Happy Face]] *instructs* the mask (put it on); **Great Time to Be Human shows the mask become a group activity — a whole civilization agreeing to grin.** It is the Happy Face scaled from a face to a species.

## Crooks and believers, the same song

The hinge line is small and total: *"The crooks and believers all sing the same song."* Crooks and believers are supposed to be **opposites** — the whole moral order depends on the difference. The spectacle dissolves it: under the cheer, the liar and the faithful **function identically**, because what matters is not what you mean but that you're singing along. This is [[City of Angels]]'s *"everyone looks just like you"* moved from the visual register to the **moral** one. `[Strong Read.]`

That collapse is worth a name, because the catalogue otherwise treats collective singing as *sacred* — the Circle, "the crowd sings it back." This song forces the counter-case, and the vault now holds it as [[Communion vs Spectacle]]: **communion is many distinct voices singing the truth together; spectacle is many voices forced into the same song.** The crowd here is not awakened. It is **synchronized.** A unison is not a communion.

## A grotesque nursery

The form is the giveaway. *"There once was a boy… There once was a girl…"* is the opening of a fable, a bedtime story — exactly the **"twisted takes on adolescent musical themes for hands that rock the cradle"** the album announces of itself. And the capitalized nouns aren't transcription noise: *Mother, Man, Lover, The Fountain, The Mountain, Someone, They, The Chemist* are **stations**, not people — the song is a miniature anthropology. `[Grounded capitalization; archetypal reading Strong.]`

Read as a diptych, the two fables map a small catalogue of failed systems — kinship and religion (the boy: mother, brother, lover, preacher), then youth, medicine, and creation (the girl: fountain, mountain, institution, chemist, god). And they fail **asymmetrically.** The **boy matures into repetition** — he becomes Man and runs to a Lover who *"cast a spell to bring back his Mother,"* so his adult love is conscripted to re-animate the dead parent: grief routed into recurrence rather than healed. The **girl is dispossessed** — she loses her youth, goes back for it, and is punished for it (*"they had her committed and shaved off her hair"*). Masculine becoming as repeated trauma; feminine becoming as theft and punishment. `[Strong Read.]`

That spell-casting Lover is the album's **false feminine** again: the partner asked to resurrect the past rather than restore the man to wholeness — the counterfeit that re-animates the wound where [[Mary]] would heal it. And the preacher who *"banged a gong"* is ceremony without transformation: ritual noise that performs meaning on cue. The *"bloodsuckers with crocodile shoes"* who throw *"a party with diamonds and spoons"* are predator and consumer at once — any institution that profits from the wounded body and then dresses the wound as a celebration.

## The god made by mistake

And then the song's sharpest image: *"She went to The Chemist to make her a dog / But he was dyslexic, so he made a god / God, god, god (Woof!)."* This is the album's whole **mirror-logic** condensed into a joke. She asks for a **dog** — a companion, creaturely love, something humble to be loved by. The broken maker hands her a **god** instead — abstraction, authority, worship — and he does it **by error**, a typo, a dyslexic's slip. Divinity manufactured without understanding: **failed alchemy**, elevation with no soul in it. `[Grounded text; Strong reading.]`

The genius is the *"(Woof!)."* The made-god still **barks** — the dog is trapped inside the god, the reversal collapsing in both directions at once: dog becomes god, and god is exposed as dog. It is a perfect emblem of the album's **counterfeit divinity** — kin to Lucius the false light, to City of Angels' false immortality, to Lucius counterfeiting Mary. A people that misreads its own need for creaturely love and builds an idol instead. `[~]` In a Gnostic key it is the **Demiurge as clerical error** — the botched lower maker who mistakes himself for the source; framing god-making as *dyslexia* mocks the whole idea of divine intention.

## The empty mirror, and the laugh track

Roy wears a tattoo called *The Empty Mirror* — "looking at yourself and seeing nothing." This song *is* that operation. The cheer reflects back only the **desire** — rainbows, butterflies, bliss — while the **reality** of the fables goes entirely un-reflected. Look into the song's smile and you see nothing of what just happened in it. `[Strong Read.]`

Even the ad-libs enforce it. *"Oops! / yay! / boo hoo / Woof!"* are children's-show emotional cues, fired at exactly the **wrong moments** — a brother is smothered and the song says *oops*; kids dance to destroy and it says *yay.* They are reaction-buttons: the sound of horror being processed into **content**, and content needing applause. The algorithm laughing at the boy who lost his mother. And when a single exit finally appears — *"Shanghai Summertime / Goodbye"* — it is immediately reabsorbed into the chorus. Even leaving is swallowed by the cheer-machine. There is no way out of the pageant; *"everything keeps blooming"* whether you heal or not, because **growth is not the same as healing.**

## Whose grin?

The relentless positivity is Lucius-mode — but the song is too observant, too grief-conscious, to be *pure* Lucius. The truest framing is that **Lucius is staging Roy's lament as a children's spectacle**: the content knows it's horrifying; the form refuses to stop smiling. Both voices are on the credit. The song is the argument between them, performed as a sing-along. `[Strong Read — do not flatten to pure Lucius.]`
